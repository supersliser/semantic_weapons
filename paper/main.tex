\begin{abstract}
This project presents a new method of approaching AI assisted 3D content generation by taking advantage of the \gls{semantic} nature of of \glspl{llm} to create a set of parameters which are fed into an implicit surface function, such which can then be remeshed to a useable and adaptable mesh based object for use in other 3D \gls{dcc} applications. This paper will study: the need for such an application, the place for it in the industry and how such a technology can be achieved.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{intro}
With the development and popularisation of \gls{llm}, specifically its generative capabilities. Many enthusiasts and researchers have studied the possibility of its expansion into the domain of Computer Graphics. This has been explored through many avenues such as: The use of python based vector graphics and HTML where the \gls{llm} generates the code used for this; The generation of bitmap based images based on a \gls{semantic} description; And many have attempted to take advantage of the node-based approach to AI (\gls{neuralnetwork}) in order to allow it to generate 3D models. The latter of which has been found to be the least successful and stable.

\subsection{What is Artificial Intelligence?}

There are multiple ways of understanding the definiton of an AI System. The first is a purely ideological standpoint. This approach is largely based upon the work by Alan Turing. Wherein by "In its strictest definition, AI stands for the imitation by computers of the intelligence inherent in humans" \parencite{sheikh2023artificial}. Even in 1997, AI was being defined by ``Representations of ``reality,'' cognition, and information'' \parencite{national1997computer}. In this sense AI is any system in which the line between machine and human is blurred, where a person may not be able to tell the difference. There are many issues with this definition however. Firstly, it is based upon the computer-literacy of the user. Somebody who is more familiar with computer systems will be much more likely to be able to spot an AI system than somebody who lack such skills. Secondly, there is the intention of the algorithm, an algorithm for a chatbot will always be more capable of seeming \textit{human} than a calculator, as the chatbot's goal is to, well, chat, something that has always been quite specific to humans.

There are also more specific versions of this understanding. Concepts such as AI existing to "capture of the thinking of a recognized expert, especially in the case where the expert is about to be lost." \parencite{simmons1988artificial} or the ability for "learning, reasoning and self-correction" \parencite{kok2009artificial}. These allow us to determine the defintion of an AI based on its features. This however, presents the issue of simulation. The name "Artifical intelligence" implies that the machine is innately intelligent, even if artificial. The intelligence is manufactured but is still present, however, there is a question of the system \textit{simulating} this intelligence. If it appears to be acting with intelligence but without actual intelligence, then is it intelligent? Is a simulation of a reality equivelant of said reality? This question poses an issue to these specific, functionality based definitions of AI as it does not provide a clear distinction between \textbf{intelligence} and \textbf{acting intelligent}.

A further way of defining AI could be by its technical design. By associating it with other terms such as "Expert Systems" \parencite{simmons1988artificial} or "Neural Networks" (the concept of such being explored in \cite{dobrev2012definition}). However, this removes the understanding of an AI outside of the realm of computer science \parencite{abbass2021artificial} and limits it only to this field.

For the purposes of this paper, AI will be defined in the way in which the UK Government explain the phenomenon in \cite{gajjar2023artificial} where ``Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies are capable of:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item interpreting, processing and generating realistic human-like speech and text
    \item interpreting, processing and generating images, videos and other visuals
    \item independently performing tasks in the real world, such as when they are paired with machinery such as robots
\end{enumerate}
''

This definition has been chosen as it is not overly specific to the functionality, without being too abstract that it could include an overwhelming number of concepts. Furthermore, its agreement by the legal background of this country provides confidence in its functionality as a strong definition.

\subsection{Gen AI}

Generative AI (gen ai) is a specific functionality of artificial intelligence systems in which the system is able transform a textual prompt of \gls{semantic} information into an output with some form of creative simulation. 3 of the biggest use cases of this are: futher textual data, programming code and even images or video. For the purposes of this exploration, I first wish to focus upon programming code.

Within the realm of programming, the tools used to code a system have gone through multiple generations of development. Beginning with generation 1 programming language, A.K.A. Machine code. This was the rawest form of creating an intended output, however was too cumbersome and difficult to program with efficiently. Thus, the transition to generation 2 programming languages, nicknamed ``Assembly'' was born. This system would allow those with less education on the subject to make more with less effort, less understanding and less time. Although, at this stage the barrier to entry was still exceptionally high, such that anyone without the education afforded by a specialised institution would not be able to attempt programming.

Due to this, still incredible, barrier to entry, 3rd generation programming languages were created to allow much easier input into computer systems, providing direct and abstract interfaces for lovelacian principles, this made programming much more accessible, such that it was simple enough to allow learning through experience or other methods of self-study. However, this required the simplification and abstraction of many principles within the field. With this switch, it could be described that the focus shifted from \textit{how the computer works} to \textit{how the program works}. This change was then pushed further with the advent of 4th generation languages which focussed on \textit{what the program does} rather than \textit{how the program works}, evidencing further accessibility at the reduction of difficulty and control.

Finally, I propose that the generative AI systems being able to write 3rd generation code could be considered as a 5th generation language. By way that it is an abstraction of language features to provide simplicity and lower the barrier to entry for programming. However, as with all previous generations, the code that is generated by this 5th generation language will never be as efficient as the code that results from languages of a lower generation, it will always be slower and more generic, but at the cost of taking less time to write. This occured when moving from assembly to a compiled 3rd generation language, and again when moving to AI written code (5th generation).

This progression can be seen in other realms of creation as well. For example computer graphics. Originally, computer graphics were only able to be generated by computer scientists and people with extreme skill and knowledge on the subject. Specialists in computer science with specific training in the subject; it was in its 1st generation state. Afterwards, libraries such as OpenGL, Vulkan, Metal and DirectX could act as an ease of use interface, simulating the 2nd generation. 3rd generation could then be considered the advent of 3D \gls{dcc} tools such as Maya and houdini. These transformed the workflow in a near identical way to how the 3rd generation languages transformed the domain of programming. Furthermore, as with programming languages, an understanding of the programming behind CG (1st and 2nd generation) would massively given benfit towards the use of Maya (the 3rd gen) in the sense that you can optimise things to work better. However, while said opimisations may be essential in the 1st generation, they are perhaps optional in the 3rd.

\subsection{The ``so what''}

As a result of all of this, it makes natural sense that many would seek to allow a form of AI integration with this workflow to bring 3D CG into its own 5th generation. And as with 5th generation programming, it is unrealistic to expect it to be completely stable or provide the most opimised results. This is how we avoid the percieved moral issues with AI replacing human workers \parencite{luccioni2020morality} \parencite{xie2025artificial} \parencite{eng2024will}. When 3rd generation programming languages were introduced, the value of the skills of knowing 2nd and 1st generation languages was not lost, if anything the value of the skill was made more apparent by the fact that most could create an application, but very few could create an \textit{efficient} application. And by such, the programmers with the skill to optimise beyond a 3rd generation language would remain in demand. When 5th generation language began to take over, the value of those who could use 3rd generation language was not lost, simply transformed, they became more valuable assets in comparison to the ``anyone'' who could use a 5th generation language. This is because when the 5th generation language resulted in an issue, only the 3rd gen experts would be able to solve it.

The wish of AI assisted computer graphics is not for the skills of those using Maya and \gls{dcc} tools to become lost souls with no work whom were replaced by AI, rather, their job should become increasingly important and simply take less time as the ``easy parts'' can be done by the 5th generation language, leaving optimisation and bug-fixing to the 3rd generation experts.

\section{Background} \label{background}

\subsection{Generative AI meets 3D} \label{back1}

\cite{li2023generative}

As I mentioned in section \ref{intro}, the field of Aritifial Intelligence has been attempting to combine with 3D computer graphics for some time. This piece discusses the various ways in which Gen AI is being trained, and the types of data being used to train them for this purpose. The most important distinction it makes between data types is the difference between \textbf{structured} and \textbf{non-structured} data. Where structured data includes voxels and multi-view images due to their grid-like representation, and non-structured data includes 3D meshes and point clouds due to their lack of organisation within the structure.

The thing that stands out most to me is how these systems to do not appear to consider some form of intermediary system, they fail to take into account that an \gls{llm} is ultimately a \gls{semantic} system; They would prefer to input text and just get a 3D model without an intermediary system to help the translation. They also seem remarkably generic, focussing on a system that can generate any model. As has been found time and time again in the realm of programming (\cite{mernik2005and}, \cite{negm2019survey}, \cite{hennessy2019new}, \cite{brown2011heterogeneous}, \cite{gerofi2017toward}), specialised systems will always outperform systems designed with no clear purpose in mind, and they will be outperformed in terms of results, speed, energy efficieny and even complexity. As such, an AI system specifically designed to build 1 type of 3D object (melee weaponry in this case), will be superior to a system that tries to do \textit{everything}.

\subsection{Introduction to implicit surfaces} \label{back2}

\cite{bloomenthal1997introduction}

As is discussed in section \ref{back1}, there are many ways in which computer systems can understand an object in 3D spaces. However, one way which doesnt seem to have been considered is that of an SDF, also known as an implicit surface. In the "Introduction to implicit surfaces", we get an exploration in to the basics behind this purely mathematical representation of 3D objects. We are shown that any object can be expressed as a function of a point in space\footnote{It is worth noting, this does not have to be 3D, as the book describes that the point should simply be represented as the point \textit{P} as it can then work in other dimensionalities such as 2D or 5D if necessary.}, where this function equalling 0 is representative of the surface of the object. One of the benefits of this system is that it retains the benefits of a vectorised representation over a bitmap representation of an image in 2D space \parencite{graphics2005vector}. In such, the mesh method of representing a 3D object could be considered comparable to a bitmap due to its lack of infinite scaling posibility. A capacity which is held by implicit surfaces. The other main benefit (especially beneficial for our purposes), is that it can be stored in a purely mathematical format, thus allowing for textual storage and representation of an object.

\section{Implementation} \label{impl}

The intended technology was the implementation of a system which can generate a 3D model of a weapon from the description of a character. This system would originally have 2 distinct stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Converting the character description to a set of parameters
    \item Converting the parameters to a 3D object
\end{enumerate}

My intention was that only item 1 would involve the use of artificial intelligence as I believed that better results could be achieved with a predefined formula for a weapon, especially knowing the basic template of the object being some form of sword. This massively decreases the level of adaptability that the system needs to be capable of performing.

\subsection{Three Staged Approach}

As mentioned in section \ref{impl}, the original intention was to utilise 2 distinct stages, however, this was under the impression that the system would utilise direct mesh generation / editing. It was only on a further analysis that the use of implicit surfaces became a more effective possibility, such which would require a 3rd stage to act as an intermediary between the 2 preexistant ones. The purpose behind separating these into 3 separate modules in the first place is due to the vast differences in technology that these use. Where the first stage must use some form of \gls{llm}, the middle stage can use ai or be done using a preset list of conversions prepared within the executable binary, and the final stage is simply the execution of an SDF binary which is predetermined to guarentee stability. 

The other purpose of keeping these separate is to enable hot-swapping adaptability. It is noticeable that all 3 processes input and output through standard file types. The Description AI takes in a standart text input and outputs in a JSON file format, a recognised ascii type used all across web data transfer. The Conversion AI inputs and outputs in this same JSON format and the final stage allows for the input of a file or individual values. As a result of this, other alternative methods can be used in place of each of these, such as: alternative models, manual conversion between \gls{ordinal} and \gls{nominal} types, or even other formulas to create different implicit surfaces. This system allows for adaptability to future systems or developments.

\subsubsection{Description AI} \label{descAI}

The first stage of the process is to convert the \gls{semantic} description to a set of \gls{ordinal} parameters. This is stored using a standard JSON format. This is largely dependent upon prompt crafting to have the output be generated in the correct format. The binary created for this project makes use of the \textit{Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct} model \parencite{meta_llama32_2024}, however, for the sake of accessibility for those without access to \gls{tpu} technology, the binary also allows for direct outputting of the prompt to allow the user to copy and paste to other \glspl{llm}.

See appendix \ref{descPrompt} for the full system prompt. The user prompt is not present as this is where the character description is inserted. This prompt was built to provide as much context to the AI as possible without allowing it the possibility of mistaking description with instruction. While the prompt states that the output should only contain the JSON format, some models struggle with this concept. This necessitates human intervention for the output to be correctly formatted.

\subsubsection{Conversion System} \label{convAI}

This is the feature which was determined to be needed after determining that section \ref{weapongenerator} would utilise implicit surfaces rather than meshes. The concept is that there must be some form of conversion between the \gls{ordinal} parameters outputted from the system in section \ref{descPrompt} and the \gls{nominal} data needed for the formula computed in section \ref{weapongenerator}. Two approaches to this system were prepared and tested: one in which the \gls{ordinal} parameters were handed to another \gls{llm} to be mapped to the required \gls{nominal} parameters (see appendix \ref{convPrompt} for full prompt). The other method was manual mapping, where in which each \gls{ordinal} parameter would be mapped to a set of mathematical operations for selected \gls{nominal} parameters such that the default blade is edited to meet the intended input.

After testing both systems, ultimately the manual method was found to be more successful due to stability (more explanation can be found in section \ref{reswith})

\subsubsection{Implicit Surface Generator} \label{weapongenerator}

The final stage in this process is to utilise the \gls{nominal} parameters within a predefined sword formula. This exists in the form of a mathematical function which takes 3 parameters by default: x, y and z. This system is where most of the development time went in, as there is little technological development in the realm of implicit surfaces, largely due to the mathematical requirements of such systems which limits the capacity of most artists to truly express their artistic vision. As such the formula was written using trial-and-error until a satisfactory base was created.

The sword\footnote{For the purposes of this program, any weapon where the blade protrudes parallel to the handle is considered a sword. Thus, it includes spears and other such items} itself can be thought to be able to be split into three primary components:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The handle (a cylinder of variable length, with an optional pommel on the bottom)
    \item The hand guard (a piece of shaped metal used to separate the handle and blade, protects the wielders hands from harm)
    \item The blade (a sharpened tool which actually does the cutting, usually encased in a scabard until use for protection of the bearer)
\end{enumerate}

All three of these can be customised individually to form many unique patterns for the sword. As such: the blade can be curved or straight, be one sided or two, be long or short and be wide or thin. Within the realm of the guard, there is even greated customisation capability with the shape, curvature, size and other such parameters. Thus is makes sense

\subsubsection{Remeshing}

While it is not an integral part of the functionality of the program. A concern for many use cases of such a tool would be compatability with other \gls{dcc} tools, most of which would not have direct support for implict surface functions. Thus, it is necessary for the accessibility of the program to be able to convert it to a mesh that can be used in any other tool or techonology. The most frequently used algorithm for such a \textit{remeshing} task is known as "Marching Cubes" \parencite{lorensen1987marching}. This algorithm has issues with preserving sharp edges and details, an extremely important feature for a program for generating swords. Thus a different algorithm with better precision on these sharp details was necessary. Ultimately, an algorithm named "Manifold Duel Contouring" was selected instead for its strength with handling sharper details and edges \parencite{schaefer2007manifold}.
